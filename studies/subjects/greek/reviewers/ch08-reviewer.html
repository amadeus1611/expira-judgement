Step 1 · §8.1

A preposition links two words

"The book is under the table." Under states how book relates to table. The word after the preposition is its object. The preposition, its object and any modifiers make up a prepositional phrase.

Even in English the object takes a special form: "under him", never "under he". Greek takes the same idea further.

Check
  1. In "John came with his disciples", what is the object? disciples (the phrase is "with his disciples")
  2. Why is "under he" wrong? The object takes the objective case (him), not the subjective (he).

If English puts the object in a special form, what does Greek do with that form? Step 2.

Step 2 · §8.3, 8.4, 8.7, 8.8

In Greek the case of the object picks the meaning

A Greek preposition's object is in the genitive, dative or accusative, and that case decides what the preposition means. διά + genitive means "through". διά + accusative means "on account of".

  • The object almost always comes right after the preposition.
  • The object is never nominative, except in rare cases.
  • Strictly, the preposition governs the case of its object. When translating, though, you read the case first and get the meaning from it (§8.3, footnote 1).
Learn each meaning as a pair (§8.7): "ἐν with the dative means in."
To explain a case (§8.8): "αὐτῷ is in the dative because it is the object of the preposition ἐν, which takes the dative."

So make one flashcard for each preposition-and-case pair (§8.4), not one per preposition.

Check
  1. What decides the meaning of διά? The case of its object.
  2. Which case never follows a preposition? The nominative (apart from rare exceptions).
  3. How many cards does διά need? Two: one for the genitive, one for the accusative.

With that rule, nine prepositions sort themselves by how many cases each takes. Step 3.

Pattern to notice (my summary of the table): every genitive meaning that answers "from where?" (ἐκ, ἀπό, παρά) is a genitive. Motion toward a place is accusative (εἰς, πρός). Being at rest in a place is dative (ἐν, παρά+dat). Not every meaning fits: διά+gen and μετά+gen don't. BBG makes a related point: the accusative meanings are the ones that "can normally be graphed" in space.

BBG notes two things about objects. The object of μετά+gen is usually a person; σύν is used for impersonal objects. The object of ὑπό is usually a person too (footnotes 16 and 22).

Check (say the full formula aloud)
  1. ὑπό + accusative under
  2. ὑπό + genitive by
  3. μετά + accusative after
  4. παρά + dative beside, in the presence of
  5. Name the "from" opposite of εἰς, and of πρός. ἐκ; ἀπό
  6. Which case does ἐν take? Dative only.

Now that you know the meanings, put them into English. Step 3 also changes how you handle the genitive and dative key words. Step 4.

Step 4 · §8.5, 8.16, 8.17

Translating a phrase

  1. No key word (§8.5). A genitive or dative that is the object of a preposition does not take "of" or "to". The preposition supplies the meaning.
    ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ = the word of God (genitive of possession, so the key word is used).
    ὁ λόγος ἀπὸ θεοῦ = the word from God. Not "from of God".
  2. Treat the phrase as one unit (§8.16). When you split a sentence into parts, keep the phrase together and find the word it modifies, usually the verb: ὁ λόγος / ἔρχεται / εἰς τὸν κόσμον.
  3. Add the article back if needed (§8.17). Greek often drops the article in a phrase: εἰς κόσμον can be "into the world".
Check
  1. ἐκ τοῦ οἴκου out of (from) the house. No "of".
  2. ἐν τῇ θαλάσσῃ in the sea. No "to".
  3. μετὰ τοῦ ὄχλου with the crowd
  4. μετὰ τὰς ἡμέρας after the days
  5. ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ by God

Prepositions never inflect, so the only time a preposition's form changes is because of the sound of the word after it. Step 5.

Step 5 · §8.6, vocab footnotes

Elision: prepositions don't inflect, but they shorten

παρά is παρά with any case. The only change has to do with sound. When the preposition ends in a vowel and the next word starts with one, the final vowel drops and an apostrophe takes its place (elision, cf. §4.2). If the next word also has rough breathing, the consonant before the apostrophe changes too, because the combination is easier to pronounce.

The rule: before rough breathing, π becomes φ and τ becomes θ. ἀλλά (but) is a conjunction, not a preposition, but it elides the same way.

ἐκ / ἐξ works differently: there is no apostrophe. It is ἐξ before a vowel and ἐκ before a consonant. BBG explains that the real form is ἐξ (ξ = "ks" + s), and the s drops between consonants.

Check
  1. μετὰ ἡμῶν becomes μεθ’ ἡμῶν
  2. ἀπὸ αὐτοῦ becomes ἀπ’ αὐτοῦ (smooth breathing, so π stays)
  3. You see ὑφ’. What is the full form? ὑπό
  4. ἐκ + οἴκου ἐξ οἴκου

Elision fixes two vowels meeting by removing one. Greek has a second fix for the same problem: adding a consonant. That fix leads to εἰμί. Step 6.

Step 6 · §8.13

Movable ν: the other fix for two vowels meeting

A word ending in a vowel can add a ν when the next word starts with a vowel: εἰσιν αὐτοί. The ν separates the two vowel sounds, much as English "a" becomes "an". In Koine it sometimes appears before consonants too, especially on dative plurals. You only need to recognize it, not decide when to use it.

Elision removes a vowel: μετ’ αὐτόν. Movable ν adds a consonant: ἐστιν ἐν.
Check
  1. Why does ἐστί(ν) have the ν in parentheses? It is a movable ν: it may or may not appear.
  2. Do you ever see εἰσί without the ν? No. In our texts the ν is always there.

To read ἐστί(ν) and εἰσίν you need verb endings and person. Step 7.

Step 7 · §8.10, 8.11

Just enough verb grammar: stem, ending, person

A verb is made of a stem, which carries its meaning, plus a personal ending, which shows person and number. There are three persons, the same ones you met in the pronouns: ἐγώ (1st, I), σύ (2nd, you), αὐτός (3rd, he, she, it). Each has a singular and a plural. γράφεις = you write; γράφει = he writes. A verb agrees with its subject in person and number.

Verbs are covered properly from ch. 15 (§8.10). Chapter 8 teaches only εἰμί, because it is the most common verb.

Check
  1. What two things does a personal ending tell you? Person and number.
  2. What does "agreement" mean here? The verb's ending has the same person and number as its subject.

Use those six slots (three persons, singular and plural) to learn εἰμί. Step 8.

Past: ἦν = he/she/it was. It is common, so learn it now. Lexical meanings: I am, exist, live, am present.

Memory aid (mine, not in BBG): the plural forms all start with ἐσ-/εἰσ-. The ending vowel tells you the person: -μεν = we, -τε = you all, -σιν = they. Don't mix up ἐστί (is, 3rd sg) with ἐστέ (you all are, 2nd pl).
Check (then type them in the drill)
  1. we are ἐσμέν
  2. εἶ you (sg) are
  3. they are εἰσίν
  4. ἐστέ you (pl) are
  5. he was ἦν

εἰμί doesn't act on anything, so the noun after it can't be a direct object. Step 9 covers what it is.

Step 9 · §8.2, 8.15

Predicate nominative

A noun after "to be" receives no action. It tells you something about the subject, so it is in the subject's case, the nominative. English works the same way: strictly, "the teacher is I", not "me" (§8.2). This is the second use of the nominative, after the subject.

θεὸς ἐστὶν κύριος: both nouns are nominative. Context shows which one is the subject.

Check
  1. Translate ὁ θάνατος ἐστιν ὁ ἐχθρός. Death is the enemy. ἐχθρός (enemy) isn't in ch. 8 vocab; I added it as a practice word. Both nouns are nominative.
  2. Why is κύριος nominative in the BBG example? It is a predicate nominative after εἰμί: it describes the subject and receives no action.

Now a full sentence can have a subject, εἰμί and a predicate nominative. Step 10 shows how to find that main core when clauses get in the way.

Step 10 · §8.9

Dependent clauses

ἵνα (in order that, that) and ὅτι (ch. 6) always begin a dependent clause. A dependent clause cannot stand on its own: "if I go home" needs "I will eat dinner". It has its own subject and verb, but the main subject and verb of the sentence are never inside a dependent clause. When you divide a sentence, set aside the dependent clauses and the prepositional phrases first (§8.16). What's left is the main clause.

The chapter's Exegetical Insight on 1 Cor 5:5 shows why this matters. Paul introduces the result with εἰς and the purpose with ἵνα, which suggests the purpose of the discipline is restoration.

Check
  1. Which words in this chapter open dependent clauses? ἵνα (ch. 8) and ὅτι (ch. 6).
  2. Where will you never find the main verb? Inside a dependent clause, and never inside a prepositional phrase as its object.
